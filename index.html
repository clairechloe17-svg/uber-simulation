Section 01 — The Business Problem

Why forecasting is Uber's
most important algorithm

Every time you open Uber and see "2 min away," that is not luck — it is the output of a demand forecasting model that ran hours before you even thought about needing a ride. Uber's entire business depends on having the right number of drivers in the right place at the right time: too few drivers and riders wait, get frustrated, and delete the app; too many drivers and they sit idle, earn nothing, and stop accepting shifts. The forecasting model is what sits between those two failure modes. It reads historical trip patterns, identifies when and where demand spikes — Friday nights, concert let-outs, snowstorms, New Year's Eve — and positions drivers in advance so the marketplace clears in seconds instead of minutes. Get the forecast right and Uber prints money. Get it wrong and the marketplace seizes.

Section 02 — The Data & The Model

Two years of Friday nights
near WWU campus

I built two years of historical Friday night Uber trip data — 104 consecutive weeks starting January 2023 — near WWU's campus in Bellingham. The dataset captures a realistic upward trend as Uber's local market matured (growing from roughly 280 trips per Friday toward 400+), summer seasonality, the school-year college-town effect (September through May running ~15% busier), a sharp late-December drop when students leave for winter break, and a New Year's Eve spike that towers above every other week at 538 trips. From that dataset I engineered four predictive features — week number, summer flag, NYE flag, and school-year flag — and trained a linear regression model on the first 78 weeks. The remaining 26 weeks became a blind test set the model had never seen.

Friday Night Uber Trips Near WWU — 2 Years of History

The upward trend is visible across the full two years — early 2023 school-year Fridays cluster in the 320–350 range while late 2024 school-year Fridays cluster in the 406–434 range. The New Year's Eve spike on December 29, 2023 is the undisputed peak at 538 trips — more than 40% above the weekly average. The December dips flanking each NYE spike confirm the school-break demand collapse. Summer weeks are elevated but not as high as school-year weeks, because the festival/tourist lift partially offsets the absence of WWU students — but doesn't fully replace them.

Backtest Results — Actual vs. Predicted (Weeks 79–104, Jul–Dec 2024)

The dashed line is what the model predicted. The solid line is what actually happened. The gap between them is the model's error — and the honest measure of how much to trust it.

The model tracked the six-month test period with an MAE of 25.4 trips — just 6.8% of average weekly demand, which falls in the acceptable range for operational driver dispatch planning. Two divergences stand out. In early August, actual demand spiked to 454 trips while the model predicted 401 — a 53-trip miss almost certainly driven by a real-world event (a festival, large outdoor gathering, or local surge) for which the model had no feature. In late December, the model predicted 432 trips but only 317 materialized — the model underestimated how far demand collapses when WWU students go home for winter break. Both gaps are honest reminders of what a four-feature model cannot see.

Section 04 — Stress-Testing the Forecast

How fragile is
the recommendation?

Before sending that memo, I stress-tested the key assumptions to understand how fragile the recommendation was.

The surge call rests on two assumptions the model cannot verify: what fraction of the evening's trips concentrate in the post-concert rush hour, and which demand scenario (conservative, base, peak) actually materializes. The table below shows how the driver count and surge decision shift as those inputs change.

Scenario Rush-Hour % Demand Case Rush Trips Drivers Needed Surge?
Base case ★ 30% Predicted (425) ~128 43 YES
Conservative 30% Lower bound (361) ~108 36 YES
Peak night 30% Upper bound (489) ~147 49 YES
Dispersed crowd 20% Predicted (425) ~85 29 YES
⚠ Flip row 20% Lower bound (361) ~72 24 NO ← flips

Conclusion: The surge recommendation holds across four of five scenarios. The only combination that flips the call to NO is a 20% rush-hour concentration paired with lower-bound demand — meaning the concert crowd disperses slowly (food trucks, merch lines, lingering outside) rather than exiting all at once, AND demand comes in at the low end of the forecast range. The operations manager should activate surge preemptively at 9:45 PM and monitor live queue depth; cancelling surge if the crowd disperses is far cheaper than scrambling for supply after a demand spike with only 25 drivers online.

Stress-testing is not pessimism — it is professional due diligence. A real analyst runs sensitivity analysis before committing to a recommendation because the assumptions baked into a model are educated guesses, not facts. If a recommendation flips from YES to NO when a single assumption moves by 10 percentage points, the operations manager needs to know that before pre-positioning forty-nine drivers and activating surge pricing across the campus zone.

Section 05 — When the Algorithm Decides

The gap between
prediction and decision

In practice, Uber's system does not hand a forecast number to a human manager and wait for a decision — it feeds that number directly into a pricing algorithm that sets the surge multiplier automatically, with no human review in the loop. When the model is right, this is fast and efficient: prices rise, more drivers come online, the queue clears, everyone goes home. When the model is wrong — say, an earthquake empties a stadium at 8 PM instead of 10 PM, or a sudden storm grounds every outdoor event simultaneously — the algorithm still executes on a forecast that is now fiction, and real people on both sides of the marketplace pay the price in stranded riders, misdirected drivers, and automatically inflated fares that feel predatory in a moment of crisis. Automation compresses the time between a bad prediction and a bad outcome to nearly zero.

The model does not know it is wrong. It just outputs a number. What happens next depends entirely on the system that consumes that number — and whether a human is still in that loop.

Section 06 — When the Model Breaks

Distribution shift and
the Bellingham scenario

⚠ Model Failure Mode

Six weeks after the concert, WWU enrollment declined sharply, new apartments opened in the Cordata neighborhood pulling residential demand north of campus, and Lyft expanded its Bellingham driver network — and the model had absolutely no idea any of it happened. It kept predicting Friday night demand as though nothing had changed, because from its perspective nothing had: it was still reading the same four features against the same learned coefficients, trained on a world that no longer existed. Drivers earned less than expected because fewer trips materialized in their assigned zones. Riders waited longer than the app promised or quietly switched to Lyft. Nobody received an error message. Nobody was told why. The model just kept running, confidently wrong, until someone noticed the revenue numbers and decided to investigate.

Section 07 — My Analysis

What I actually learned
building this from scratch

When data is input into the system, the model is trained to predict future demand patterns accordingly. Later, the model will direct drivers to certain areas based on where it predicts demand will be greatest. If the model expects the demand to be high, it will drive up prices, so customers wind up paying more because of data that was collected six weeks before. Meanwhile, the drivers depend on the model to direct them to the most profitable locations, where they can maximize the number of rides they give and therefore, how much they make.

If I were building this forecasting system for a real Bellingham business, I would add safety alerts for the drivers. Similar to how the system orchestrator would receive alerts about safety hazards driving up demand — as discussed previously in the system — the drivers would then be notified that they are going into a potentially dangerous situation.
